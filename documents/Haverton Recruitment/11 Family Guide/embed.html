A plain-English guide for families in England: what care at home typically costs, how to pay for it, whether your council can help, and where to get free advice.

This is general information to help you plan, not financial or legal advice, and not a price list. Costs vary by area and provider, and the rules can change. Always get a free needs assessment from your council and check current figures on GOV.UK before making decisions.

1. What care at home typically costs

Care at home is usually charged by the hour, so the cost depends on how many visits you need and how long they last. A short daily visit costs far less than several long visits a day.

Hourly rates vary a lot by area and provider, and tend to be higher in London and the South East, and for evenings, weekends and complex needs. Live-in care (a carer living in the home) is usually charged as a weekly fee instead of by the hour.

Because prices differ so much locally, the honest advice is: ask two or three local providers for their current rates, and get a written quote based on the actual visits you need. Don't rely on a single national "average".

2. The main ways to pay

RouteIn short
Your councilIf your needs and finances qualify, your local authority may arrange or contribute to your care. Start with a free needs assessment (see below).
NHS Continuing HealthcareIf care is needed mainly because of a health condition, the NHS may fund it in full. Ask about a CHC assessment.
Paying for it yourselfMany families arrange and pay privately, either because their savings are above the limit or because they want to choose their own provider.
BenefitsSome benefits are not means tested and can help with the cost — see section 5.
Direct paymentsIf the council agrees to fund your care, you can often take the money as a direct payment and arrange care yourself.

3. The means test explained

If you ask the council for help, they look at your income and savings. In England for 2026/27 the limits are:

Savings and capitalWhat it usually means
Above £23,250 (upper limit)You normally pay for your own care.
Between £14,250 and £23,250The council may help, but you contribute. For every £250 above £14,250 you are treated as having £1 a week of "tariff income" towards your care.
Below £14,250 (lower limit)Your savings are not counted; you contribute only from your income, and the council provides the most support.
Good to know for care at home: when you are cared for in your own home, the value of that home is not counted in the means test. Councils can also apply a more generous limit for home care than for a care home. Your council will explain how this works for you.

4. Start with a free assessment

Anyone can ask their local council for a needs assessment under the Care Act 2014. It is free, and you do not have to be on a low income to have one. It looks at what help you need day to day, and it is the gateway to any council funding.

If money is involved, the council then does a separate financial assessment (the means test above). You can ask for both. Contact your local council's adult social care team to begin, or start at gov.uk/apply-needs-assessment-social-services.

5. Benefits that can help

  • Attendance Allowance — for people over State Pension age who need help with care. It is not means tested and you can spend it on care. There are two weekly rates depending on how much help you need.
  • Personal Independence Payment (PIP) — for people under State Pension age with care or mobility needs.
  • Carer's Allowance — for a family member who provides regular unpaid care.

Amounts change each year, so check the current rates and how to claim on GOV.UK.

6. Where to get free, independent advice

  • Your local council's adult social care team — assessments and local options.
  • Age UK — free advice line and clear guides: ageuk.org.uk.
  • Citizens Advice — free help with benefits and money: citizensadvice.org.uk.
  • MoneyHelper (government-backed): moneyhelper.org.uk.
  • GOV.UK — official rules: gov.uk/care-support-you-can-get.
  • For complex finances, consider a specialist care-fees financial adviser (look for SOLLA accreditation).
